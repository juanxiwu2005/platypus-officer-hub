# R1: Learning-science review of the 3-day officer onboarding draft

Scope: evidence on how adults learn long policy material, retrieval and practice design, curiosity, incentives, mandatory training, and newcomer socialization, checked against DRAFT.md. All citations below were found by web search on 2026-11-21. Where only the abstract or secondary coverage was reachable, the note says so. Bauer's 2010 SHRM "4 C's" report was confirmed only through SHRM-hosted and secondary pages; its exact wording is unverified. A Klein & Polin 2012 handbook chapter was not verified and is not used.

## Key findings (short)

1. **People do not read long policy text, and reading alone is a weak study method.** In a 543-student experiment, most participants agreed to absurd terms of service (Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch 2018). Web users read about 20% of the words on a page (Nielsen 2008, practitioner source). Rereading and highlighting are rated "low utility" (Dunlosky et al. 2013). Do not treat "pages read" as learning.
2. **Retrieval practice works.** Testing beats restudy on delayed tests (Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Meta-analyses: g ≈ 0.50 (Rowland 2014); 118 articles, testing beats every comparison condition (Adesope et al. 2017). Spaced practice is the other "high utility" technique (Dunlosky 2013; Cepeda et al. 2006).
3. **Front-loaded rules decay if they are not used.** Skill loss grows with nonuse, and cognitive or accuracy tasks decay faster (Arthur et al. 1998). Performance support given at the moment of the task did as well as or better than training alone on procedural tasks, though the studies are small (Nguyen & Klein 2008). This supports just-in-time lookup for rare rules.
4. **Pretests help even when the learner gets them wrong.** Failed retrieval before reading improved later learning (Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009; reviewed in Pan & Carpenter 2023, with the caveat that effects depend on the procedure). Curiosity comes from a felt knowledge gap (Loewenstein 1994). A curious state also improves memory for nearby incidental material (Gruber et al. 2014).
5. **Rewards for doing or finishing undermine intrinsic motivation.** In free-choice settings, engagement-contingent rewards gave d = −0.40 and completion-contingent rewards d = −0.36 (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999). Badges and leaderboards lowered motivation over a semester (Hanus & Fox 2015). Points raised the quantity of output but not intrinsic motivation (Mekler et al. 2017). Overall, gamification has small positive effects on cognitive outcomes, and social interaction is a moderator (Sailer & Homner 2020). Effects depend heavily on context (Hamari et al. 2014).
6. **Mandatory, check-the-box training often fails to change behavior.** In an 8-month RCT at a real organization, completing annual awareness training did not predict whether employees clicked simulated phishing links (Ho et al. 2025). Harassment training builds knowledge more than it transfers to behavior (Roehling et al. 2022; finding from secondary summary). Mandatory enrollment weakens the link between mastery motivation and transfer (Gegenfurtner et al. 2016). Cognitive learning from training is durable, and effects are larger when training is embedded in other initiatives (Bezrukova et al. 2016).
7. **Newcomers adjust through role clarity, self-efficacy, and social acceptance.** These three predict performance and retention (Bauer et al. 2007, 70 samples). Onboarding that stops at "compliance + clarification" is the low end; culture and connection matter more (Bauer 2010, unverified wording). Inform, Welcome, and Guide practices are the recommended categories (Klein, Polin & Sutton 2015).
8. **Unguarded AI can act as a crutch.** In a study of about 1,000 students, unrestricted GPT raised practice scores but lowered exam scores by 17%. A tutor version with guardrails removed the harm (Bastani et al. 2025).

## Ranked design principles for a 3-day ramp

1. **Front-load only decision-critical triggers. Deliver everything else just in time.** Rules not used soon decay. People skip long policies. Performance support at the moment of the task works for procedures. (Arthur et al. 1998; Obar & Oeldorf-Hirsch 2018; Nguyen & Klein 2008; Nielsen 2008)
2. **Learn by retrieving with scenarios, not by reading. Count mastery, not opens.** (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Adesope et al. 2017; Rowland 2014; Dunlosky et al. 2013)
3. **Open each node with a guess-first question to create an information gap.** (Richland et al. 2009; Pan & Carpenter 2023; Loewenstein 1994; Gruber et al. 2014)
4. **Give corrective feedback that names the decisive condition, then retry with a related new case. Track confidence.** (Butler & Roediger 2008; Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001)
5. **Sequence worked example → partial → changed scenario. Let experienced officers test out.** (Sweller & Cooper 1985; Kalyuga et al. 2003; Brunmair & Richter 2019 for interleaving confusable rules)
6. **Space the re-checks: day 1 → day 3 → the first real trigger → about 2–4 weeks.** (Cepeda et al. 2006; Rawson & Dunlosky successive relearning)
7. **Build role clarity, self-efficacy, and connection early.** One real task and the human handoff happen in the first 48 hours. "Who to ask for what" should be explicit. (Bauer et al. 2007; Klein, Polin & Sutton 2015; Bauer 2010)
8. **Motivate through competence, autonomy, and relatedness, not points for consumption. Use AI as a guarded tutor while learning and as a direct, sourced assistant while doing.** (Ryan & Deci 2000; Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999; Hanus & Fox 2015; Sailer & Homner 2020; Bastani et al. 2025)
